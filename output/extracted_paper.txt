INTRODUCTION 
 Buildings are continuously exposed to natural hazards such as earthquakes and 
typhoons, making structural resilience an important consideration in building planning and 
design. Earthquakes can impose sudden ground-motion demands on structural systems, while 
typhoons generate strong winds that can damage roofs, walls, openings, and other building 
components. Because these hazards affect buildings in different ways, evaluating resilience 
requires consideration of both hazard intensity and building characteris tics. Recent multi -
hazard research emphasizes the importance of considering earthquake and wind hazards 
together rather than evaluating a building against only one dominant hazard. Studies found 
that a building designed primarily for one hazard may not nec essarily perform satisfactorily 
against another, demonstrating the importance of multi-hazard assessment.  
This issue is particularly relevant in the Philippines, where buildings are exposed to 
frequent tropical cyclones and significant seismic hazards. Re search identified building 
components and building typologies that are important for assessing vulnerability to extreme 
wind loads, noting that severe -to-complete damage has occurred particularly in non -
engineered low-rise buildings. Similarly, post -event research on the 2013 Bohol earthquake  
used ground -motion simulations and a database of more than 25,000 damaged and 
undamaged structures to develop and constrain building fragility and vulnerability models  
(Bautista et. al., 2017).  
However, these approach es are generally assessment methodologies, vulnerability 
models, or specialized analytical frameworks , rather than a simplified web -based system that 
accepts common building/site inputs and translates the resulting risk assessment into 
preliminary design recommendations. 

=== PAGE 3 ===
Earthquake and typhoon vulnerability can be quantitatively assessed using established 
mathematical models.  Buildings can be measured by how fragile it is if there was an 
earthquake. Similarly, Philippine studies have shown that low-rise buildings are vulnerable to 
typhoon-induced winds and have developed building classifications and empirical wind 
vulnerability curves based on wind speed and observed damage. Moreover, multi -hazard 
assessment frameworks emphasize the importance of considering  both earthquake and wind 
hazards, since the dominant risk can vary depending on the building’s characteristics, site 
conditions, and performance requirements. These studies support the use of mathematical 
models to evaluate and compare the resilience of buildings against multiple hazards. 
Existing studies have developed sophisticated models for individual hazards. 
However, there remains a need for a student-accessible computational framework that 
integrates earthquake and typhoon vulnerability into a unified building-level assessment.  
This study aims to develop a web -based mathematical model that evaluates the 
earthquake and typhoon resilience of a proposed building based on selected site and building  
characteristics and generates preliminary design recomm endations based on the resulting risk 
assessment. Specifically, the model will integrate established mathematical approaches for 
seismic hazard/vulnerability, wind or typhoon vulnerability, spatial/site risk, and multi -
criteria weighting. The resulting risk values will be converted into a resilience index and used 
by a rule -based recommendation component to identify building characteristics that may 
require improvement. The system is intended as a preliminary mathematical decision-support 
tool, not as a rep lacement for professional structural engineering design or code -compliant 
structural calculations. 
 

=== PAGE 4 ===
 The main objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a web -based 
mathematical model that assesses building resilience against earthquake and typhoon hazards 
and generates corresponding design recommendations. The proposed model is designed to 
integrate relevant site and building characteristics, apply established mathematical and 
computational models to determine hazard and vulnerability levels, and provide a resilience 
index that can guide preliminary building design decisions. Specifically, this study seeks to 
answer the following questions: 
1. What site and building characteristics are significant inputs for assessing building 
resilience against earthquake and typhoon hazards?  
2. How can established mathematical models for earthquake and typhoon hazards be 
integrated into a web-based mathematical model for building resilience assessment?  
3. How can the proposed model determine an overall Building Resilience Index from the 
calculated earthquake and typhoon risk factors?  
4. How do changes in site and building characteristics affect the resulting Building 
Resilience Index?  
5. How can the calcula ted risk levels and resilience index be used to generate 
appropriate preliminary design recommendations for improving building resilience?

FRAMEWORK 
Mathematical Concepts 
The first mathematical component of the model will be GIS buffer and spatial 
analysis, which will be used to determine the proximity of the proposed building site to 
identified active fault lines. The geographic coordinates of the proposed building location 
will be compared with the location of active faults, and a spatial distance  analysis will be 
performed. The distance obtained from the GIS analysis can be used as a site -related factor in 
the earthquake assessment. The purpose of this component is to identify whether the 
proposed building is located relatively close to an active fault and therefore requires greater 
consideration in the seismic assessment. 
The second mathematical component will be the Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) 
model, which will represent the intensity of expected earthquake ground motion at the 
proposed building site. PGA will be used as the primary numerical indicator for the 
earthquake hazard component. The model will obtain the necessary seismic parameters, such 
as earthquake magnitude, distance from the seismic source, and site or soil characteristics, 
and use an ap propriate published ground -motion relationship to estimate the expected PGA. 
The resulting PGA value will then be converted into a standardized earthquake hazard score 
so that it can be combined with the typhoon hazard score.

Data Collection 
The data collection phase will involve gathering the site, geographic, seismic, wind, 
and building characteristics required by the m athematical model. Site information will 
include the location of the proposed building, lot dimensions, soil type, and distance or 
proximity to active fault lines. Geographic coordinates will be used for GIS processing and 
fault-proximity analysis. Active fault information will be obtained from appropriate 
authoritative geological or seismic sources, while soil information will be collected from 
available site or geotechnical information appropriate to the study. 
Building information will include the propos ed building height, number of floors, building 
dimensions, building configuration, building materials, and roof type. These variables will be 
converted into the numerical or categorical formats required by the mathematical model. The 
building characteristi cs will be particularly important in the wind assessment because 
building geometry, height, exposure, and other characteristics influence the calculated wind 
demand. 
Seismic and wind -related data will also be collected for the mathematical calculations. 
Earthquake-related information will include the parameters required by the selected PGA or 
ground-motion model, while wind-related information will include the design wind speed and 
the applicable site and building parameters required by the NSCP wind provis ions. The 
collected data will be checked for consistency, completeness, units, and compatibility with 
the equations used in the model.

Exploratory and Spatial Data Analysis 
Before the mathematical calculations are performed, the collected data will undergo  
exploratory and spatial analysis. Numerical variables such as building height, lot dimensions, 
distance from faults, PGA, and calculated wind pressure will be examined to identify 
missing, inconsistent, or unrealistic values. Categorical variables such as  soil type, building 
material, roof type, and building configuration will be encoded into appropriate numerical 
categories where required by the model. 
GIS analysis will also be conducted to visualize the relationship between the proposed 
building location  and active fault lines. The spatial analysis will provide a geographical 
representation of fault proximity and will support the earthquake -risk assessment. The 
purpose of this stage is to ensure that the data entering the mathematical model are properly 
prepared and that the spatial relationships relevant to the study are correctly represented. 
Model Development 
The model development phase will integrate the different mathematical components 
into one web -based system. The first stage of the computational p rocess will receive the 
proposed building location and perform GIS -based fault -proximity analysis. The resulting 
spatial information will then be used together with the required seismic parameters for the 
PGA calculation. 
The earthquake component will calc ulate the expected PGA using the selected 
ground-motion relationship. The calculated PGA will then be converted into an earthquake 
hazard score using the predefined normalization or scoring function. The resulting earthquake 
score will become one of the two primary hazard indicators used by the overall model. 

=== PAGE 10 ===
The wind or typhoon component will process the building and site characteristics 
required by the NSCP wind provisions. The system will calculate the applicable wind 
pressure or wind demand and subsequently transform the result into a standardized typhoon 
hazard score. This will provide the second primary hazard indicator of the model. 
The resulting o verall score will be classified according to predetermined risk or 
resilience categories established by the researchers. The classification thresholds must be 
defined before evaluation and supported by the methodology rather than being arbitrarily 
selected after observing the results. 
The final computational stage will be the AI-based recommendation generation 
process. The system will provide the calculat ed earthquake and typhoon hazard scores 
together with the relevant proposed building characteristics to an artificial intelligence model. 
The AI model will analyze these inputs and generate preliminary building resilience 
recommendations based on the ident ified hazard conditions and the characteristics of the 
proposed building. 
Model Validation and Evaluation 
The developed mathematical model will undergo validation to determine whether its 
calculations are correctly implemented and whether the outputs behave consistently wi th the 
mathematical relationships used. Individual calculations for distance, PGA, wind pressure, 
hazard scores, and the overall risk or resilience index will be tested using known or manually 
calculated sample cases. The web -based implementation will then  be checked against these 
reference calculations to determine whether the system produces the expected results. 
Sensitivity analysis will also be performed to determine how changes in the input 
variables affect the resulting hazard and resilience scores. Selected variables, such as distance 

=== PAGE 11 ===
from the seismic source, soil condition, building height, building dimensions, and wind 
speed, may be varied while keeping other variables constant. The resulting changes in the 
model outputs will be examined to determin e whether the system responds logically to 
changes in important input parameters. 
The recommendation component will also be evaluated by testing different 
combinations of earthquake and typhoon hazard levels and observing whether the system 
produces the co rresponding predefined recommendations. The web -based system itself will 
additionally be evaluated in terms of functionality, calculation accuracy, consistency of 
outputs, and usability. These evaluations will help determine whether the proposed 
mathematical model operates according to its intended computational framework.
